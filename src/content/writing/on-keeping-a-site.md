## A Statement

It's easy to be pessimistic about tech these days. Many of my close friends regularly talk about moving back to a "90's" lifestyle, but I don't really agree with that as an answer, and I don't think that many of my close friends would enjoy going back to that world.

What I (and I suspect my friends) want is less bullshit. [No predatory data collection, no manipulative app practices, no "uncancellable" fees etc.](https://deceptive.design/) I want an internet that helps all people. Not just some multibillionaire rich asshole who's looking to profit off of our time and interests. I've spent 12 years now in digital strategy and SaaS and have built a career that requires me to know how to track and manipulate users, and to be frank, I think this is a terrible waste of an incredible technology.

This site is intended as a deliberate statement against centralized "social media", and a call to revisit the intent and promise of the web itself as a platform.

## On Nostalgia

In recent years, there's been a revivalist attitude around "web 1.0." The "pre-social media" age. Many folks in their 20's and 30's have fond recollections of animated GIFs, web rings and guestbooks, and sites like Neocities cater to that nostalgia. I'm very fond of it and the intention behind the movement, but I don't think it serves me all that seriously.

After years of moving through web trends (geocities, blogger, digg, tumblr, etc.), the idea of jumping onto a specific platform and riding another wave of growth, stagnation, and decline just doesn't appeal to me. With Neocities in particular, I think it's anchoring itself to a very specific era and people who were active on the internet in the late 90's/early aughts. I think it'd be a mistake for people to once again commit to a specific platform on the web as opposed to the web itself.

## Federation

I'm much more interested in the ongoing efforts to (re-)decentralize the web as well as communication platforms like LoRa where (in theory) we might even be able to [cut cable providers out of the equation](https://www.nycmesh.net/) as well and create a [robust](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.03465v1), free, and democratic means of public communication.

Fediverse platforms are a good step in this direction, but they seem to me to be a small facet of what the web COULD be. They're more like an adaptation of old concepts grafted onto modern attitudes, and less like a complete revolution in and of themselves. Mastodon in particular is a very cool platform (that I plan to continue to advocate for!), but it's clearly coming out of a desire to replicate twitter rather than achieve a loftier goal of broad, self-owned communication. (NOTE: I do have to give props to [ActivityPub](https://www.w3.org/TR/activitypub/), the W3C spec that Mastodon is built on).

If we're going to take inspiration from the past, I'd rather take from web founder Tim Berners-Lee who has [often spoken of the web's unfulfilled potential.](https://webfoundation.org/2019/03/web-birthday-30/) I'd like to imagine a more human-centric internet where anyone can have a website, host their content and materials there and feed it out RSS style to whomever wants it, while maintaining total control of their personal data and content. (Berners-Lee's [Solid](https://solidproject.org/about) is a perfect example of a truly useful methodology that genuinely empowers users).

## The Future

[An individual website is under my control. No platform owns it.](https://indieweb.org/) I can design it however I want, or just grab a simple template and write what I want. I don't have to worry about being "flagged" or having my account banned (on my own server), and the majority of issues that plagued the early web (media sharing, [post distribution](https://indieweb.org/POSSE), accessibility) have long since vanished from concern for me.

Individual sites are not a panacea. We still require and rely on mirrors and caches to ensure that records exist when sites and data vanish, and the verification of source is still (and will likely always be) a huge concern. The biggest concern I imagine most people will point out is discovery. While I acknowledge the fear that useful things will never be found, I'd argue that the system we have now is significantly worse than what we had prior to the advent of centralized algorithmic recommendations and more broadly the "social graph" of facebook, livejournal, blogger etc. Word of mouth might be slow and hard to monetize, but it's authentic to human experience and a little more resilient against outside influence.

We don't need these platforms anymore: they're middlemen. We can and should close out "social media" as a concept. They were a very effective lure to get more people online, but the platforms themselves have proved deeply untrustworthy and self-aggrandizing. I would love to try to build up technology that focuses more on helping people better themselves and others than provides addictive distractions and puts money in someone's pocket.

If we as the public aren't intentional with the systems we build and use, we offer up control to whomever can leverage those systems against us. I want a humanist internet, an intentional one. Not one bloated for its own sake.
